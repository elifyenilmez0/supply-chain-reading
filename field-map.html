Supply chains: an OM–economics field map

Modified

October 20, 2026

NoteCurrent scope

The main question is supply chains through OM and economics. Of the original 38, 33 are in the supply-chain focus and 5 are optional general methods/framework background. ORLM and the broad Dai–Swaminathan framework are not supply-chain-specific core readings. SPO, Bertsimas–Kallus and Ban–Rudin are methodological references whose relevance depends on a particular inventory/sourcing question.

See the unchanged original Read closely 19 and where their notes/highlights are. The original priority and current relevance are separate labels. The historical 38-item register is retained below for traceability.

What I am trying to understand

I started with a fairly broad question: supply chains have been studied for a long time, so what are OM and economics researchers trying to understand now? I am using these 38 readings to work out the connections before choosing a project. AI is part of that question, but it plays quite different roles across the papers.

The list mixes papers, books and shorter articles. They are useful for different reasons. The coverage table at the end records what was available; the “Read closely” label is my original priority, not a claim that I have finished every paper.

My current picture

My reading of this selection is that the interesting questions connect operating choices, changing production networks and wider economic consequences. The underlying decisions—inventory, sourcing, information sharing and capacity—are established OM questions. What changes is the environment, what can be observed, and which consequences a study needs to explain.

I would distinguish three developments. First, firms adapt their supplier relationships and production locations in response to uncertainty and policy, so the network cannot always be held fixed. Second, researchers can sometimes connect transactions, shipping and accounting data to observe those adjustments, although access and measurement remain major constraints. Third, AI changes the cost of prediction, formulation and interaction, while creating new workflow and physical-infrastructure problems. These developments create questions at the OM–economics boundary; they do not make all older inventory or sourcing research obsolete.

This is a synthesis of a deliberately selected reading list and a small current-literature check, not a bibliometric ranking of the whole field. This list is strong on production networks, trade, sourcing and decision methods. It is thin on labor, contracts in AI supply chains, climate adaptation, circularity, humanitarian operations, transport and causal evaluations of actual AI adoption. Those are omissions to address before selecting a topic, not evidence that they are unimportant.

Papers can belong to several areas. Each item is assigned one primary area below for navigation, not to imply separate disciplines or mutually exclusive methods. IE, OR, OM and economics overlap. The distinction I need is the question and contribution: a faster algorithm, an operating policy, an empirically established mechanism or an equilibrium/welfare result.

The connections I should be able to explain

1. From propagation to network choice

Network Origins explains why asymmetric input links can make local shocks aggregate. Carvalho supplies empirical evidence of transmission after an actual earthquake. Beyond Hulten shows why nonlinearities and substitution matter beyond first-order accounting. Inoue and Guan provide simulation-based approaches with different units and assumptions.

The newer mechanism papers then ask why firms choose the links and reliability they do. Elliott studies reliability thresholds and investment; Acemoglu–Tahbaz-Salehi studies relationship gains, bargaining and link dissolution; Kopytov studies ex ante technique choice under uncertainty. These are complementary explanations, not three estimates of the same effect. Kopytov’s efficient flight to safety is a useful counterexample to the idea that every output loss indicates excessive private fragility.

OM bridge: observe a specific operating choice—supplier retention, stock, qualification or capacity—and ask whether it changes the transmission or recovery mechanism. A network centrality score by itself is not a policy, and adding a familiar decision to a large network is not automatically a new contribution.

2. From direct trade flows to operational adjustment

Alfaro–Chor documents changing direct sourcing. Dhyne shows how domestic links transmit foreign exposure. Antràs–de Gortari explains stage-level geography; Antràs–Chor explains ownership; Costinot–Vogel–Wang isolates reliability in sequential specialization. These distinguish where, who controls, and what input is needed.

Niu makes the OM bridge more tangible by observing supplier, country and shipment adjustments. Jain connects sourcing with working capital and recovery. Fajgelbaum and Caliendo–Parro connect policy costs to economic incidence and welfare. I would not use any one of these to assert that reshoring universally increases resilience.

OM bridge: study the adjustment path and its consequences—qualified capacity, delivery performance, inventories and total cost—rather than simply count announcements or countries.

3. Why resilience findings can appear to disagree

“Resilience” may mean fewer interruptions, less lost production during an interruption, faster recovery, lower financial volatility or higher expected welfare. Those outcomes can move differently. Diversification may reduce an exposure and still weaken volume incentives or make recovery coordination harder. Inventory can absorb a short interruption but not a long one. A nearby source helps only if its relevant capability is available.

The Jain recovery evidence is an association based on an inferred recovery measure, not a general causal law against diversification. The JPE and QJE models identify conditional incentive differences; they do not say any particular firm has chosen badly. I need to name the outcome and benchmark before using “more resilient.”

4. From more information to better action

Lee–So–Tang establishes information value in a specified two-level demand setting. The Science alliance article concerns network measurement and institutional access. The related 2023 Culot methodological critique, already in the follow-up library, cautions that corporate supplier relationships need not be the relevant product flows. These are different information objects.

SPO, Ban–Rudin and Bertsimas–Kallus explain how prediction and data should be assessed through operational loss. None establishes that an inferred supplier edge is a verified alternative or that unknown constraints are covered by a known-feasible-set learning framework.

A correction worth remembering: the original Lee–So–Tang discussion should not be reduced to “higher autocorrelation always makes sharing more valuable.” Babai et al. (2016), accepted manuscript/abstract qualifies that monotonic claim. I checked this at abstract level; I have not audited the correction’s derivation.

Where AI fits

Layer What changes Evidence in this list What I can responsibly say
Prediction and contextual decisions Rich features can inform cost-sensitive decisions Ban–Rudin; Bertsimas–Kallus; SPO Established before the recent LLM wave. Better average forecast accuracy is not sufficient evidence of better operating decisions.
Formulation Natural language can be converted into a model and solver code ORLM Technical benchmarks and a limited human-assistance study support potential in specified tasks. Correct code execution does not prove the intended problem was correctly modeled.
Interaction Planners can ask what-if questions and obtain explanations around a solver OptiGuide Demonstrates a workflow and a Microsoft application. It does not establish a causal improvement in resilience or calibrated managerial trust.
Workflow, responsibility and adoption Humans need review, exception handling and clear decision rights Dai–Swaminathan Current OM framework highlights these questions. Its synthesis is not one new causal experiment.
AI’s own operations and physical supply chain Deployment needs data pipelines and monitoring; AI scale also needs chips, servers, power and cooling Dai–Swaminathan plus current MSOM/IEA sources Distinguish operating AI systems from supplying their physical infrastructure. Both offer OM questions. Physical capacity remains scarce even when prediction becomes cheaper.

My strongest distinction is AI as a method versus AI as a change in supply-chain decisions or constraints. A new forecasting model can be a method for an old inventory problem. A firm delegating procurement decisions changes workflows and incentives. A surge in compute demand creates a capacity and investment-coordination setting. These require different evidence and may lead to different contributions.

Possible AI mechanisms to investigate include cheaper search and verification, faster formulation, changed human attention, common model errors, and coupled chip/power investments. These are research possibilities, not results inferred from the 38 papers. In particular, shared AI use causing synchronized shortages or a new bullwhip effect needs direct prior-work search and evidence before it can be called a gap.

A dated update beyond the original list

These sources update the picture as of 20 October 2026. They are supplementary context, not additional members of the original 38.

  • Alfaro–Chor, NBER 34490, November 2025, revised April 2026. The abstract reports continued US sourcing reallocation, concentrated among major partners, with adjustment spreading to relationship-sticky goods. It distinguishes China decoupling from withdrawal from world trade. This is a separate paper from the 2023 reading, not an update silently substituted for it. Primary record. Coverage: abstract/record only.
  • Cohen et al., MSOM, published online 26 March 2026. A community vision organizes AI and supply chains around intelligence, execution, strategy, people and infrastructure. I take its integration and physical-supply-chain agenda as a signal of current OM questions, not as measured adoption impact or proof of consensus across all OM research. Selected abstract, introduction and infrastructure sections consulted. Primary article.
  • IEA, Key Questions on Energy and AI, 2026. Its updated executive summary projects data-centre electricity consumption around 950 TWh in 2030, roughly 3% of global electricity demand, with near-term equipment and chip constraints. These are projections for data centres, not electricity already consumed by AI alone. I use this primary sector outlook to motivate capacity coordination and avoid repeating an unverified much higher percentage from a commentary. Executive summary. Coverage: executive summary only.

The follow-up collection also contains Nature/Nature Communications, network-information and measurement sources from the earlier targeted review. They can enrich several branches of this map; their presence does not mean the upstream-risk branch has been selected.

For each direction I need: an important observed friction, a decision maker, a counterfactual benchmark, an outcome and a realistic data route. The first-year task can be a carefully designed descriptive replication, an assumption comparison or a small model. It should help rule out a question as readily as support it.

Why I kept the broader readings

I read Econometrica, QJE, JPE and REStud for mechanisms, identification and welfare arguments. Science’s Policy Forum motivates a broad measurement institution; Nature Human Behaviour and Nature Sustainability supply modeling settings and societal outcomes; they are distinct journals from flagship Nature. The Economist and HBR help find concrete puzzles and explain why a reader should care, but are not equivalent to academic causal evidence. Books provide historical mechanisms and chronology.

For an OM contribution I still need a meaningful operating choice and a result that changes how that choice is understood. A topical setting or a broad-interest citation cannot substitute for that contribution. A buyer-level study need not estimate general equilibrium; a policy/welfare recommendation needs more than showing that a buyer’s cost falls. These are evidence standards I am learning from the papers, not predictions about where a future paper will be accepted.

Coverage and bibliographic corrections

A priority label is not an access label. The 19 close-reading notes retain their earlier source/version and scope, and now add field connections and discussion questions. The other 19 have explicitly scoped field notes. Some are selected full-text reviews; others remain abstract-level or access-limited. The table that follows documents every original item rather than hiding those limitations.

Two important metadata corrections are reflected in the current notes: On the Geography of Global Value Chains uses DOI 10.3982/ECTA15362; and the Dai–Swaminathan source is the published 2026 POM article Artificial Intelligence and Operations: A Foundational Framework of Emerging Research and Practice, DOI 10.1177/10591478251412943, 35(9), 3255–3271. Supply-chain scope versus general methods

For the AI part, the concrete connection is a supply-chain decision: for example, a planner asking what-if questions about server placement in OptiGuide, or the linked 2026 MSOM supply-chain vision. A framework spanning healthcare, general human–AI work and model development is broader background. It should not be used to expand this reading project into AI/OR generally.
