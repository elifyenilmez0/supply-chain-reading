What I want to discuss

I am still trying to work out where an interesting OM question might be. These readings helped me separate a few things that I had been putting together: how shocks spread, how firms change their sourcing, and whether those changes actually improve performance. I also want to understand what AI changes in supply-chain decisions, such as sourcing, inventory and planning.

My current thought is that the basic decisions are familiar, but the connections between them are important. A sourcing decision affects inventory and recovery. It can also affect firms further along the network. AI may make analysis easier while leaving capacity, incentives and implementation as the difficult parts.

I have not picked a topic yet. For this meeting, I mainly want to check whether I am connecting the papers sensibly and decide where to read further.

Comparisons to remember

  • Acemoglu–Tahbaz-Salehi and Kopytov: not every move toward a safer network is a market failure. I need to distinguish output from welfare.
  • Alfaro–Chor, Dhyne and Niu: direct trade flows, indirect exposure and firm-level sourcing actions show different parts of the story.
  • The two Jain papers: inventory investment and recovery speed are different outcomes. Diversification need not help both.
  • Carvalho, Inoue and Guan: evidence around an actual shock is different from a simulation under chosen assumptions.
  • AI in a supply-chain decision: OptiGuide provides a concrete application. SPO, Ban and Bertsimas are method references; ORLM and the broad AI–OM framework are optional background, not the core reading agenda.

Read closely: notes to talk through

The links below have the fuller notes, source versions and reading coverage. Some local PDFs are earlier working papers; Tomlin is still abstract-only. I want to explain the mechanism before trying to remember the numbers.

1. The Macroeconomics of Supply Chain Disruptions

A supplier relationship is worth more than the input being bought. Both sides have put work into making it useful. If one link stops being worthwhile, losing it can make other links unattractive too. What I need to understand is why the firms let this happen even when keeping more of the network would be better overall.

What makes the privately chosen network inefficient?

A buyer and supplier do not capture the full benefit their link creates elsewhere. The distinction is between choosing the network and operating on a given network; I should not say every production-network model is inefficient.

Kopytov’s efficient choice of safer techniques is a different mechanism; Elliott studies reliability thresholds in deep networks.

Paper notes.

2. Endogenous Production Networks Under Supply Chain Uncertainty

Choosing safer suppliers can mean giving up some productivity. The part I want to remember is that this can be a sensible trade-off, not necessarily a mistake. Lower output and lower welfare are not the same thing here.

How should I compare the output loss with the gain in stability?

A risk-averse household values stability. Lower expected GDP and lower welfare are different claims. The welfare benchmark matters before proposing a subsidy.

Acemoglu–Tahbaz-Salehi emphasizes a network externality; this paper shows that a move toward safety need not itself be a market failure.

Paper notes.

3. Supply Chain Resilience: Should Policy Promote International Diversification or Reshoring?

I expected this to make a simple case for more diversification. It is less straightforward: a firm benefits from keeping its own product available, but it also benefits if its competitors fail. Those incentives make the policy answer depend on the model.

What exactly would justify a diversification subsidy here?

The consulted working paper gives conditional results. Under CES demand and restricted instruments diversification can be the constrained-optimal intervention; broader demand assumptions can change its direction. I would not recommend universal reshoring.

Tomlin asks what one buyer should do; this paper asks whether private choices line up with social welfare.

Paper notes.

4. Global Supply Chains: The Looming Great Reallocation

The country a shipment comes from does not tell me the whole production story. US sourcing can move away from China while Chinese inputs or investment remain important in the new locations. I should separate a change in direct trade from a change in underlying dependence.

How far can direct import data tell us about decoupling?

It documents reallocation of direct sourcing and several related indicators. I would separate decoupling from China from a general retreat from trade, then ask whether indirect exposure also changed.

Niu looks at firm–product sourcing actions; Dhyne exposes indirect foreign dependence. The newer Alfaro–Chor paper is a separate 2025/2026 follow-up.

Paper notes.

5. Supply Network Formation and Fragility

Having alternatives for one input is useful, but the firm still needs all its essential inputs. In a deep network, a small deterioration across many relationships can therefore matter a lot. I still need to get more comfortable with the threshold argument and how much carries over to a small, finite network.

How should I interpret the threshold result for a real, finite network?

It establishes a threshold mechanism in a large/deep-network model. Applying it requires finite-network data and a check of its essential-input and failure assumptions.

Network Origins has amplification on a given input–output network; this paper adds reliability and strategic relationship investment.

Paper notes.

6. Risks and Global Supply Chains: What We Know and What We Need to Know

The question is not just whether global supply chains have risk. It is whether they have too much risk relative to the gains from specialization. I find that a better starting point than assuming shorter or domestic chains must be safer.

Which parts of this agenda have moved forward since the survey?

The survey helps define questions, but its early-pandemic gaps must be checked against later work. I would investigate recovery dynamics, information quality and incentives rather than repeat its 2021 agenda as new.

This survey organizes the economic question; Tomlin and base–surge provide operational benchmarks, while later network papers formalize different mechanisms.

Paper notes.

7. Supply Chain Disruptions: Evidence from the Great East Japan Earthquake

Firms outside the earthquake area were still affected through their suppliers and customers. That makes the network exposure concrete. For an OM question, I would then want to know which choices made before the earthquake helped a firm cope or recover.

What does the earthquake identify, and what would still be needed to study sourcing choices?

It helps identify exposure to the disaster, not random assignment of sourcing policy. Better-managed firms may both diversify and recover faster. Spillovers also complicate the control group.

Inoue–Todo simulates propagation on a large network; this paper estimates effects around an actual shock. Guan simulates lockdown scenarios.

Paper notes.

8. Trade and Domestic Production Networks

A firm does not need to import directly to depend on foreign inputs. Its domestic suppliers can bring that exposure into the chain. This is why I should be careful about using domestic purchasing as a measure of independence.

What do we learn from the firm network that a sector table would miss?

It uses Belgian firm-to-firm transactions and trade records and models supplier choice. The micro network reveals heterogeneous indirect exposure hidden in sector averages.

Alfaro–Chor shows direct sourcing shifts; this paper explains why indirect links change their interpretation.

Paper notes.

9. The Return to Protectionism

The important result for me is who paid for the 2018 tariffs: duty-inclusive import prices rose, so US import buyers bore substantial costs. The gross loss to buyers is different from the net loss after producer gains and tariff revenue. I should not mix those numbers.

How much of the tariff result might carry over to a different episode?

Only as evidence from that episode. Pass-through depends on product markets, time horizon and adjustment. I would not assume the 2018 estimate is a universal 2026 parameter.

Caliendo–Parro is a quantitative tariff/welfare benchmark; Niu studies actual sourcing restructuring; Jain links sourcing to inventory.

Paper notes.

10. The Network Origins of Aggregate Fluctuations

Small shocks do not always average out across the economy. Some sectors supply so many others, directly and indirectly, that their shocks matter much more. This is the basic network idea I need before moving to the newer papers.

What do the newer network papers add to this starting point?

The propagation foundation is older. The newer questions in this list concern link formation, adaptation, private versus social resilience and richer transaction data. I should describe that progression rather than call the whole field new.

Baqaee–Farhi adds nonlinear response beyond a first-order approximation; Elliott adds essential-input reliability and endogenous investment.

Paper notes.

11. Restructuring Global Supply Chains: Navigating Challenges of the COVID-19 Pandemic and Beyond

This is a useful place to start with what firms actually did. Adding suppliers, changing sourcing countries and changing shipment sizes are different responses. The next question is which response helped performance; observing a change alone does not answer that.

Which firm decisions can the shipment data actually reveal?

The matched sample has 1,533 listed US importers, 4,444 HS6 products and 284,017 firm–product–quarter observations. Shipment size and frequency are observed; exact safety-stock targets, tier-2 changes and causal resilience benefits are not.

Alfaro–Chor provides the trade picture; Jain provides inventory and recovery outcomes. Together they suggest measuring the consequences of particular actions.

Paper notes.

12. Managing Global Sourcing: Inventory Performance

I need to distinguish the number of units in inventory from the money tied up in them. Global sourcing can mean longer lead times and more units, but cheaper purchasing prices. In this sample the stock effect dominates. It makes the link between sourcing and working capital quite clear.

How much can I interpret these inventory estimates causally?

The local working paper uses matched sea-import and accounting data with simultaneous-equation assumptions. I would explain the mechanism and qualify the sample-specific estimates.

The recovery paper studies a different outcome and finds diversification associated with slower recovery. This does not contradict a lower inventory burden in a different setting.

Paper notes.

13. On the Value of Mitigation and Contingency Strategies for Managing Supply Chain Disruption Risks

A stock buffer and a backup supplier do not protect against the same problems. Stock may bridge a short outage and run out in a long one. A backup is useful only if it can increase supply when needed. I only have the abstract here, so I still need the full model before using its policy results.

Why can two suppliers with similar uptime call for different policies?

A stock buffer can bridge short failures yet run out during a long failure. A responsive backup depends on available volume flexibility. I have checked the publisher abstract; I would not claim to have verified the full policy theorem.

Base–surge balances cost and responsiveness to demand; Tomlin explicitly treats supplier disruption. Kopytov moves reliability choice to an economy-wide welfare framework.

Paper notes.

14. Smart Predict, then Optimize — original selection; method background

A small prediction error can be costly if it changes the decision, while a larger error may leave the decision unchanged. SPO trains with that distinction in mind. The known-feasible-set assumption is something I need to watch if I try to use it for uncertain supply capacity.

Would uncertain backup capacity fit the original SPO framework?

The base framework predicts linear objective coefficients on a known feasible set. Missing capacity or unknown constraints are not automatically covered. I must check that the mathematical representation matches the real uncertainty.

Ban–Rudin learns feature-dependent inventory actions; Bertsimas–Kallus uses covariates in conditional optimization. ORLM instead produces a formulation/code; OptiGuide supports questions around a solver.

Paper notes.

15. Chinese firms are wrapping their supply chains around the globe

Moving a factory abroad does not mean moving away from its existing production ecosystem. Ownership, equipment and intermediate inputs can still connect it to China. I would use the examples to motivate a question, then look for firmer evidence in the trade papers.

What would distinguish relocation from a change in upstream dependence?

Actual local production, Chinese ownership and Chinese intermediate inputs can coexist. Transshipment is a separate claim requiring evidence.

Use alongside Alfaro–Chor’s trade evidence and Niu’s sourcing actions, with clear distinctions in evidentiary strength.

Paper notes.

16. Building an alliance to map global supply networks

Before deciding what to do with a supply network, we need to know what we can actually observe. VAT, payment and shipping data show different parts of it. The difficult part is not only filling in missing links: a link still tells us little about the relevant product or available capacity.

Which of these network data could we realistically access?

Administrative and bank microdata require access arrangements. Commercial data may be selective. A firm-level edge also does not reveal the actual part or spare capacity.

Lee–So–Tang studies the value of a specific demand signal; this article concerns network observability. SPO provides one way to think about decision value.

Paper notes.

17. Outside the Box: How Globalization Changed from Moving Stuff to Spreading Ideas

The selected chapters help me see why a cheap purchase is not always a cheap supply chain. Transport and coordination made longer chains possible, but inventories, disruption and hidden suppliers could change the total cost. The historical cases are useful examples, not estimates I can directly put into a model.

How could I use a historical case without treating it as causal evidence?

The selected chapter’s Riken or BMW coating example shows how an inexpensive upstream input can constrain costly downstream production. I would use it to motivate a question, then turn to empirical or theoretical evidence.

The Box and Humble Hero explain transport standardization; recent network papers explain mechanisms and consequences more formally.

Paper notes.

18. Global supply-chain effects of COVID-19 control measures

Restarting one region may not be enough if its suppliers are still constrained. The duration and sequence of recovery matter. I need to remember that the large losses here come from model scenarios, not a direct measurement of what COVID caused.

How should I interpret the large losses in the scenarios?

They are outcomes of 39 idealized scenarios under the model’s constraints and recovery assumptions. They illustrate sensitivity to duration and spread rather than directly measure a realized causal treatment effect.

Carvalho studies an actual shock; Inoue simulates firm-network propagation. These are distinct forms of evidence.

Paper notes.

19. The humble hero: containers have been more important for globalisation than freer trade

The container example is useful because the change is so concrete: less handling and more predictable transport made a different way of organizing production possible. It makes me think about standards and coordination, rather than assuming an important OM contribution has to be a complicated algorithm.

What evidence is behind the containerization claim?

That is the article’s framing of historical research, not a universal ranking I can apply to all periods. Adoption, ports and trade policy changed together.

Use Levinson for history and formal network/trade papers for mechanisms; read the underlying containerization study before citing its magnitudes.

Paper notes.
