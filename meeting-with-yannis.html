What I want to get out of the meeting

I want to move from “supply-chain resilience is important” to a small question where a manager makes a specific decision and the answer could change that decision. The reading suggests that supplier count, country count and purchase price can each miss something important. My strongest initial interest is in the difference between apparent diversification and actual independence.

These are research candidates, not verified literature gaps. I have reviewed selected substantive sections of the local papers for mechanisms, evidence and limitations; I have not independently checked every proof or replicated the estimates. The book note is based on selected passages. Tomlin is an abstract-only note because the full text was unavailable. The Chinese-firms note uses the accessible Economist publication on Mint, not the paid Substack continuation.

Three distinctions I should be able to explain

  1. Supplier diversity versus risk diversity. Several suppliers can share one upstream input, region or transport route. Adding a supplier need not add independent capacity. Niu et al. distinguish supplier and geographic diversification; Dhyne et al. and Baldwin–Freeman explain indirect exposure.
  2. Output stability versus welfare or profit. Kopytov et al. show a potentially efficient cost of flight to safety. Acemoglu–Tahbaz-Salehi and Elliott et al. show different mechanisms that can make decentralized networks inefficiently fragile. These are different models, not contradictory empirical estimates.
  3. A prediction versus a decision. Pichler et al. motivate learning about upstream links. SPO explains why prediction accuracy alone is not the operating objective. More accurate mapping is useful only if the buyer makes a better action, with feasibility checked separately.

1. My first candidate: when does a second supplier actually protect me?

Question. How should a buyer choose inventory and reserve backup capacity when two apparently different suppliers may share a critical upstream bottleneck, and verifying that dependence is costly?

Why it might matter. A firm could pay qualification costs and still discover during a disruption that both sources fail together. Conversely, always demanding completely independent chains could be unnecessarily expensive. The useful answer would tell the buyer when to investigate, when to hold stock, and when to pay for an independent source.

A small starting model. One buyer, one product, two regular sources and one expensive outside option. Before the shock, choose an audit, buffer stock and reserved capacity under a common budget. An audit updates the probability of shared upstream exposure; it is not assumed to reveal the whole network perfectly. After the shock, allocate the capacity actually available. Demand can initially be simple so that the information and supply mechanisms are visible.

The result I would look for. Conditions under which a third nominal source adds less value than verifying an existing source or protecting the common bottleneck. The comparison would report expected total cost, fill rate, severe-shortage probability and the value of the audit. I would compare with no-audit dual sourcing, inventory-only protection, a conservative policy and a full-information benchmark.

Data and credibility. First reproduce a small analytical/numerical example. For validation, seek a partner with a known bill of materials and a few verified upstream links. Commercial shipment links or disclosures could provide additional signals, but should not be treated as the complete network. Test nonrandom missing links and false positives. Synthetic results would demonstrate a mechanism, not establish real-world savings.

Novelty risk. Correlated failures, multitier risk, capacity reservation and information acquisition all have substantial prior literatures. Combining them is not automatically a contribution. I need to identify the closest models and show a decision reversal or a measurable value-of-information result that they do not already imply. Start centrally; competing buyers sharing the backup is a possible later extension, not an extra feature to add immediately.

2. A more empirical candidate: buffer now or change supplier?

Question. When a policy shock has uncertain duration, when should an importer front-load inventory, qualify a replacement or wait?

Decision and mechanism. Sourcing changes have sunk costs and qualification delays; inventory is quicker but ties up capital and can become obsolete. The belief that a policy will last can therefore matter as much as the current tariff. Separate announcement, implementation and realized delivery delays. Do not assume a policy coefficient from 2018 applies unchanged today.

Possible design. Use pre-event product/country exposure and a well-defined policy episode. Measure supplier entry/exit, shipment timing, inventory dollars and recovery, with event-study diagnostics. Anticipation, seasonal orders, demand changes and indirect exposure of comparison firms threaten identification. Policy assignment is not random; an event study is not by itself a causal design. A smaller descriptive replication may be the appropriate first step.

Feasibility. Panjiva-style shipment data and Compustat access would help, but must be checked before selecting the project. Public aggregate trade data can support a pilot on patterns, not identify a buyer's inventory or qualification policy. I would narrow to one industry where substitution and product perishability can be characterized.

Novelty risk. Recent Alfaro–Chor work already studies delayed reallocation in sticky and contract-intensive goods. My contribution would need to be the operational inventory/qualification trade-off or credible firm-level performance evidence, not another graph of imports shifting away from China.

3. A methods candidate: which supplier information is worth paying for?

Question. With a limited verification budget, which uncertain supplier relationships should be checked to improve an actual sourcing decision?

Approach. Start with a small sourcing problem and compare spend-based auditing, network-centrality auditing, uncertainty sampling and decision-based auditing. Include audit cost, incorrect extracted links and hard feasibility checks. If text or an LLM is used to propose links, keep the source evidence and require verification before treating capacity as real. A fluent supplier explanation is not a capacity certificate.

Contribution test. The method should improve total operating cost or shortage risk on held-out decisions, not only link-prediction accuracy. Test rare disruptions, changes in correlations and geographic shifts. The original SPO paper assumes unknown objective coefficients and a known feasible set; I cannot simply inherit its guarantees for uncertain network feasibility.

Feasibility and priority. This is attractive if we have auditable network data and methods supervision. Without those, it risks becoming a generic “AI for supply chains” project. I would use it as a precise version of candidate 1 rather than try to build a general supply-chain agent.

Recent literature check — what already overlaps

Targeted check through 20 October 2026, not a systematic literature review. The sources below were checked at publisher abstracts or overview level; none is represented as an additional fully reviewed paper.

  • Alfaro & Chor, An Anatomy of the Great Reallocation in US Supply Chain Trade, NBER 34490 — issued November 2025, revised April 2026. Its abstract describes newer U.S. sourcing shifts and the later adjustment of relationship-sticky goods. This is the immediate follow-up to the 2023 reading, so “firms reallocate slowly” is not enough for novelty.
  • Chen & Shi, Tailored Base-Surge Policies in Dual-Sourcing Inventory Systems with Demand Learning — online December 2024, Operations Research 73(4), 2025. It learns a dual-sourcing inventory policy from censored demand and gives regret guarantees relative to the best TBS policy. Learning demand with two sources is already a developed problem.
  • Chen, Chou & Sun, Process Flexibility: A Distribution-Free Approach to Long Chain Resilience — online August 2025, Operations Research 74(1), 2026. It provides distribution-free performance bounds for flexible capacity networks under disruptions. “Flexibility helps under uncertain distributions” is not an open result by itself. This paper's process-flexibility long chain should not be confused with long international shipping chains.
  • Cohen, Dai, Perakis et al., Supply Chain Management in the AI Era: A Vision Statement from the Operations Management Community — MSOM, online March 26, 2026. It emphasizes integrating learning with OM structure, reliable data and human decision making. It is an agenda/vision article, not causal evidence that an AI system improves resilience.

Small glossary for the meeting

  • Extensive margin: adding or removing a supplier link. Intensive margin: changing purchases through an existing link.
  • Domar weight: a producer's sales divided by aggregate value added/GDP; it reflects economic importance in these models and is not simply its profit share.
  • Externality: my action changes someone else's outcome in a way I do not fully count in my private decision.
  • Calibration: choosing model parameters to match selected data features. A counterfactual from that model is not automatically a causal estimate.
  • Regret: the performance gap from a specified information-rich benchmark. The benchmark must be named; it is not necessarily the unconstrained optimal policy.
  • Fill rate versus tail shortage: the average fraction of demand served can look good while a few scenarios still have very severe losses. I should measure both when the question is resilience.
