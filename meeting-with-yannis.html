My opening answer

“I initially thought supply chains were mainly an established set of inventory and logistics problems. What I am seeing in this list is a connection between operating choices and wider economic questions: how networks form and adjust, how shocks spread, when private resilience differs from social resilience, and what new data can actually tell us. AI adds both decision tools and new organizational and infrastructure questions. I have not chosen a topic yet; I want to understand which of these connections is both important and feasible to study.”

1. The Macroeconomics of Supply Chain Disruptions

Question: Why can a small shock destroy productive supply relationships?

My takeaway: I see the key change as treating the network itself as a choice. Relationship-specific gains and bargaining can make privately chosen links disappear in a cascade, even when an efficient allocation would adjust continuously.

If Yannis asks “Why is fragility inefficient here?”

A buyer and supplier do not capture the full benefit their link creates elsewhere. The distinction is between choosing the network and operating on a given network; I should not say every production-network model is inefficient.

Connection: Kopytov’s efficient choice of safer techniques is a different mechanism; Elliott studies reliability thresholds in deep networks.

Boundary: Analytical mechanism under specified complementarities; no causal estimate of AI, tariffs or supplier-support contracts.

2. Endogenous Production Networks Under Supply Chain Uncertainty

Question: How do firms rewire their input choices when uncertainty increases?

My takeaway: Safer sourcing can reduce volatility while sacrificing productivity. In this model the equilibrium trade-off can be efficient. I need to compare welfare, expected output and volatility separately.

If Yannis asks “Does lower GDP mean firms chose badly?”

Not necessarily. A risk-averse household values stability. Lower expected GDP and lower welfare are different claims. The welfare benchmark matters before proposing a subsidy.

Connection: Acemoglu–Tahbaz-Salehi emphasizes a network externality; this paper shows that a move toward safety need not itself be a market failure.

Boundary: The sectoral calibration and financial risk proxies do not directly measure a buyer’s delivery risk or qualification time.

Open detailed source note.

3. Supply Chain Resilience: Should Policy Promote International Diversification or Reshoring?

Question: Should policy encourage diversification or domestic sourcing?

My takeaway: There are competing private incentives: keeping products available helps consumers, while surviving when rivals fail can attract their demand. This is why I cannot start from the claim that firms always underinvest in resilience.

If Yannis asks “So should governments subsidize reshoring?”

The consulted working paper gives conditional results. Under CES demand and restricted instruments diversification can be the constrained-optimal intervention; broader demand assumptions can change its direction. I would not recommend universal reshoring.

Connection: Tomlin asks what one buyer should do; this paper asks whether private choices line up with social welfare.

Boundary: The local source is the June 2022 working paper, not a line-by-line check of the 2023 published version.

Open detailed source note.

4. Global Supply Chains: The Looming Great Reallocation

Question: Is US sourcing disappearing or changing its geography?

My takeaway: The useful distinction is direct import origin versus the upstream production ecosystem. A fall in direct imports from China does not by itself measure a fall in Chinese inputs or ownership elsewhere.

If Yannis asks “Is this evidence of deglobalization?”

It documents reallocation of direct sourcing and several related indicators. I would separate decoupling from China from a general retreat from trade, then ask whether indirect exposure also changed.

Connection: Niu looks at firm–product sourcing actions; Dhyne exposes indirect foreign dependence. The newer Alfaro–Chor paper is a separate 2025/2026 follow-up.

Boundary: Descriptive patterns are not a causal test of whether a particular buyer’s nearshoring improved service.

Open detailed source note.

5. Supply Network Formation and Fragility

Question: Why can a network withstand isolated failures but remain systemically fragile?

My takeaway: Alternative suppliers within one input category help, but a producer still needs every essential category. In a sufficiently deep network, reliability can change sharply near a threshold, and private investment can leave the network near it.

If Yannis asks “Does this predict an imminent real-world collapse?”

No. It establishes a threshold mechanism in a large/deep-network model. Applying it requires finite-network data and a check of its essential-input and failure assumptions.

Connection: Network Origins has amplification on a given input–output network; this paper adds reliability and strategic relationship investment.

Boundary: No measured distance of today’s economy from a collapse threshold, and no evidence that AI solves it.

Open detailed source note.

6. Risks and Global Supply Chains: What We Know and What We Need to Know

Question: Are global supply chains excessively risky, relative to their benefits?

My takeaway: I want to keep the distinction between a shock and its propagation. Resilience has an opportunity cost: specialization creates gains as well as exposure. A policy argument needs a private–social wedge.

If Yannis asks “What is still missing after this survey?”

The survey helps define questions, but its early-pandemic gaps must be checked against later work. I would investigate recovery dynamics, information quality and incentives rather than repeat its 2021 agenda as new.

Connection: This survey organizes the economic question; Tomlin and base–surge provide operational benchmarks, while later network papers formalize different mechanisms.

Boundary: Review evidence; not an original intervention estimate or a complete 2026 literature census.

Open detailed source note.

7. Supply Chain Disruptions: Evidence from the Great East Japan Earthquake

Question: Do supply links transmit a geographically localized disaster?

My takeaway: The earthquake setting makes indirect economic exposure visible. Both suppliers and customers outside the disaster zone can suffer through the network. Geographic distance is not enough to define an unaffected firm.

If Yannis asks “Is the earthquake a causal experiment for diversification?”

It helps identify exposure to the disaster, not random assignment of sourcing policy. Better-managed firms may both diversify and recover faster. Spillovers also complicate the control group.

Connection: Inoue–Todo simulates propagation on a large network; this paper estimates effects around an actual shock. Guan simulates lockdown scenarios.

Boundary: The local PDF is the December 2016 working paper. Its gross-output propagation estimate must not be relabeled as the published paper’s GDP effect.

Open detailed source note.

8. Trade and Domestic Production Networks

Question: How much foreign exposure passes through domestic firms?

My takeaway: A non-importer can still depend heavily on foreign inputs through domestic suppliers. Allowing links to change is especially important for larger counterfactual trade shocks.

If Yannis asks “Why is this more than an input–output table?”

It uses Belgian firm-to-firm transactions and trade records and models supplier choice. The micro network reveals heterogeneous indirect exposure hidden in sector averages.

Connection: Alfaro–Chor shows direct sourcing shifts; this paper explains why indirect links change their interpretation.

Boundary: Restricted administrative data; model-based foreign-price counterfactuals, not observed responses to every current policy.

Open detailed source note.

9. The Return to Protectionism

Question: Who bears tariffs, and how are gains and losses distributed?

My takeaway: In the 2018 episode, import buyers bore substantial duty-inclusive costs. Gross buyer losses, producer gains, tariff revenue and net real-income effects are different objects.

If Yannis asks “Can I use the estimated pass-through for today’s tariffs?”

Only as evidence from that episode. Pass-through depends on product markets, time horizon and adjustment. I would not assume the 2018 estimate is a universal 2026 parameter.

Connection: Caliendo–Parro is a quantitative tariff/welfare benchmark; Niu studies actual sourcing restructuring; Jain links sourcing to inventory.

Boundary: The consulted version’s $51bn gross loss and $7.2bn net loss are not interchangeable; the net estimate is statistically uncertain.

Open detailed source note.

10. The Network Origins of Aggregate Fluctuations

Question: Why do independent sector shocks sometimes fail to average out?

My takeaway: An uneven input–output structure makes some sectors influential through direct and indirect connections. Aggregate risk depends on the distribution of network influence, not simply the number of sectors or links.

If Yannis asks “What is genuinely newer if this was already known in 2012?”

The propagation foundation is older. The newer questions in this list concern link formation, adaptation, private versus social resilience and richer transaction data. I should describe that progression rather than call the whole field new.

Connection: Baqaee–Farhi adds nonlinear response beyond a first-order approximation; Elliott adds essential-input reliability and endogenous investment.

Boundary: An illustrative input–output exercise is not a sufficient-statistic measurement of every observed aggregate fluctuation.

Open detailed source note.

11. Restructuring Global Supply Chains: Navigating Challenges of the COVID-19 Pandemic and Beyond

Question: What operating actions did firms actually take during disruption?

My takeaway: Supplier diversification and country diversification are separate. Firms changed supplier portfolios, sourcing volumes and shipment patterns during 2019–2021, with differences across industries.

If Yannis asks “What exactly is observed, and what is inferred?”

The matched sample has 1,533 listed US importers, 4,444 HS6 products and 284,017 firm–product–quarter observations. Shipment size and frequency are observed; exact safety-stock targets, tier-2 changes and causal resilience benefits are not.

Connection: Alfaro–Chor provides the trade picture; Jain provides inventory and recovery outcomes. Together they suggest measuring the consequences of particular actions.

Boundary: Panjiva sea manifests plus Compustat do not cover all firms or transport modes. A published research agenda is not a verified new gap.

Open detailed source note.

12. Managing Global Sourcing: Inventory Performance

Question: How does global sourcing affect inventory investment?

My takeaway: Physical inventory and dollars tied up are different. Longer replenishment can require more units, while cheaper units offset some cost. In the consulted sample the inventory effect dominates; diversification can offset part of it.

If Yannis asks “Are these causal universal percentages?”

No. The local working paper uses matched sea-import and accounting data with simultaneous-equation assumptions. I would explain the mechanism and qualify the sample-specific estimates.

Connection: The recovery paper studies a different outcome and finds diversification associated with slower recovery. This does not contradict a lower inventory burden in a different setting.

Boundary: Pre-COVID public wholesale/retail sample and a 2012 working-paper PDF; not a current all-industry elasticity.

Open detailed source note.

13. On the Value of Mitigation and Contingency Strategies for Managing Supply Chain Disruption Risks

Question: When should a buyer prepare before a disruption versus respond afterward?

My takeaway: Inventory, reliable sourcing and contingent rerouting protect in different ways. The duration and pattern of outages matter, not just average uptime.

If Yannis asks “Why does the same uptime permit different sourcing policies?”

A stock buffer can bridge short failures yet run out during a long failure. A responsive backup depends on available volume flexibility. I have checked the publisher abstract; I would not claim to have verified the full policy theorem.

Connection: Base–surge balances cost and responsiveness to demand; Tomlin explicitly treats supplier disruption. Kopytov moves reliability choice to an economy-wide welfare framework.

Boundary: Abstract-level analysis unless an additional full text is explicitly documented. The single-strategy result has restrictive capacity/flexibility assumptions.

Open detailed source note.

14. Smart Predict, then Optimize

Question: Should training minimize prediction error or the cost of the decision it induces?

My takeaway: An error matters when it changes an action and makes that action costly. SPO changes the learning objective while preserving a predict-then-optimize deployment workflow.

If Yannis asks “Can SPO solve uncertainty about whether a backup actually exists?”

The base framework predicts linear objective coefficients on a known feasible set. Missing capacity or unknown constraints are not automatically covered. I must check that the mathematical representation matches the real uncertainty.

Connection: Ban–Rudin learns feature-dependent inventory actions; Bertsimas–Kallus uses covariates in conditional optimization. ORLM instead produces a formulation/code; OptiGuide supports questions around a solver.

Boundary: Conditional consistency results and numerical evidence do not guarantee superiority under arbitrary distribution shift or uncertain feasibility.

Open detailed source note.

15. Chinese firms are wrapping their supply chains around the globe

Question: How can Chinese production ecosystems expand abroad while direct sourcing shifts?

My takeaway: Factory country, ownership and input origin tell different stories. The article helps me avoid treating every foreign assembly site as an independent production system.

If Yannis asks “Does this prove imports from Vietnam are transshipment?”

No. Actual local production, Chinese ownership and Chinese intermediate inputs can coexist. Transshipment is a separate claim requiring evidence.

Connection: Use alongside Alfaro–Chor’s trade evidence and Niu’s sourcing actions, with clear distinctions in evidentiary strength.

Boundary: Accessible Mint syndicated article and public Substack excerpt; not the paid continuation or a causal academic study.

Open detailed source note.

16. Building an alliance to map global supply networks

Question: What would it take to observe global firm-level supply networks?

My takeaway: VAT, payment and customs records can reveal complementary portions of a network. Connecting national maps is an institutional and measurement problem as well as a technical one.

If Yannis asks “Can we simply download these data?”

Usually not. Administrative and bank microdata require access arrangements. Commercial data may be selective. A firm-level edge also does not reveal the actual part or spare capacity.

Connection: Lee–So–Tang studies the value of a specific demand signal; this article concerns network observability. SPO provides one way to think about decision value.

Boundary: Policy Forum / mapping proposal, not a newly estimated intervention or proof that complete visibility is optimal.

Open detailed source note.

17. Outside the Box: How Globalization Changed from Moving Stuff to Spreading Ideas

Question: How did globalization change from transport integration to fragmented production and ideas?

My takeaway: The historical perspective helps me see operating systems behind trade patterns: transport, coordination, inventory and hidden dependencies develop together.

If Yannis asks “What concrete example would I use?”

The selected chapter’s Riken or BMW coating example shows how an inexpensive upstream input can constrain costly downstream production. I would use it to motivate a question, then turn to empirical or theoretical evidence.

Connection: The Box and Humble Hero explain transport standardization; recent network papers explain mechanisms and consequences more formally.

Boundary: Selected introduction and chapters 14, 19 and 20; not a full reading of the 287-page book or causal verification of its cases.

18. Global supply-chain effects of COVID-19 control measures

Question: How do duration and coordination shape disruption losses?

My takeaway: Recovery depends on connected regions recovering too. A local restart may not restore production if input shortages persist elsewhere.

If Yannis asks “Are the reported losses observed pandemic GDP losses?”

No. They are outcomes of 39 idealized scenarios under the model’s constraints and recovery assumptions. They illustrate sensitivity to duration and spread rather than directly measure a realized causal treatment effect.

Connection: Carvalho studies an actual shock; Inoue simulates firm-network propagation. These are distinct forms of evidence.

Boundary: Early-pandemic scenarios; not a complete epidemiological optimization or a validated forecast for current events.

Open detailed source note.

19. The humble hero: containers have been more important for globalisation than freer trade

Question: How can an operating standard reshape international trade?

My takeaway: Containers reduced handling frictions and improved reliability, making longer production systems easier to coordinate. This reminds me to look beyond small changes in freight prices.

If Yannis asks “Were containers more important than free trade?”

That is the article’s framing of historical research, not a universal ranking I can apply to all periods. Adoption, ports and trade policy changed together.

Connection: Use Levinson for history and formal network/trade papers for mechanisms; read the underlying containerization study before citing its magnitudes.

Boundary: Complete three-page local excerpt; journalism, with the underlying empirical identification not independently checked.

Questions beyond individual papers

If he asks… My answer at this stage
“Where is the trend?” In this selection, toward endogenous networks and adaptation, measured operating responses, information/coordination and AI-enabled workflows/infrastructure. That is a qualitative map, not a count of publication growth.
“What is new about AI?” Prediction-to-decision was already established before LLMs. Formulation, natural-language interaction, task delegation and the supply chain for compute are distinct newer margins to investigate.
“What would make this OM?” A concrete feasible operating choice, a mechanism explaining its consequences and a strong comparison. A new graph or benchmark accuracy alone might be principally a methods contribution.
“Which topic have you selected?” None yet. I want to compare adaptation/recovery, information/incentives and AI-related operations, and check data access before narrowing.
“What do these papers prove about AI adoption?” The methods papers demonstrate specific capabilities; ORLM also includes a small assistance study. They do not jointly establish economy-wide or supply-chain resilience gains from adoption.
“Can we make a policy recommendation?” Only after defining social welfare and an externality. Buyer cost reduction alone does not justify a subsidy.
“Where would we get data?” Trade statistics can support descriptive exercises. Actual part qualification, capacity and decision logs may need institutional or partner access, which has not been established.
“What should happen next?” Check omissions, pick a small set of conflicting or complementary mechanisms, obtain closest full texts and do a feasibility exercise before pitching novelty.
